Introduction

Surgical diathermy, also called electrosurgery, uses high-frequency alternating current to produce controlled thermal effects in tissue. This enables tissue cutting and haemostasis during surgical procedures.

The generator, active electrode, return path, activation control, alarms and safety-monitoring systems work together as one system.

Electrosurgery differs from electrocautery. In electrosurgery, current passes through the patient. In electrocautery, a heated element transfers heat to tissue without making the patient part of the electrical circuit.

Academic scope: These simulations illustrate basic engineering principles. They are not clinical operating instructions or substitutes for equipment manuals.

Principle of surgical diathermy

Radiofrequency current, commonly in the hundreds of kilohertz to megahertz range, is used to reduce neuromuscular stimulation compared with low-frequency current. It can still cause burns and unintended tissue heating.

In monopolar electrosurgery, a small active electrode concentrates current at the treatment site. A larger dispersive return electrode spreads the returning current over a wider contact area.

  • SourceRF generator
  • FocusActive electrode
  • ReturnDispersive electrode

I = V / R    |    P = V × I = I²R

The relationship \(P=I^2R\) describes resistive power for a suitable electrical load. Actual tissue heating also depends on current distribution, waveform, tissue properties, and time.

Working

The user selects an operating mode and output setting. Activation initiates RF delivery through the selected output circuit. The return path depends on the technique: monopolar operation normally uses a dispersive return electrode, while conventional bipolar operation passes current between electrodes at the instrument tip.

Output circuit arrangements

Generator designs can differ in grounding, coupling and RF isolation. These differences affect leakage paths and electrical safety, so performance must be assessed using the relevant manufacturer instructions and applicable test procedures.

Operating modes and waveforms

Cutting generally uses a relatively continuous RF waveform, whereas coagulation commonly uses interrupted bursts. Blend modes modify the output to combine cutting and haemostatic effects.

ModeWaveform characterTypical effect
CutRelatively continuous RFSupports tissue vaporisation and separation.
CoagulationInterrupted RF burstsSupports coagulation and haemostasis.
BlendModified or interrupted cut outputCombines cutting and haemostatic effects.

Modern and automated systems

Modern generators may use microprocessor control, tissue-response sensing and output regulation. Some systems adjust output as tissue impedance changes. Features differ by manufacturer and model.

Soft, forced and spray coagulation are examples of coagulation modes. Their exact characteristics must be taken from the specific generator documentation.

ModeGeneral description
SoftContact coagulation with controlled heating.
ForcedCoagulation mode that permits more pronounced sparking than soft coagulation in relevant systems.
SprayNon-contact coagulation using electrical arcing.

Monopolar vs bipolar techniques

In monopolar operation, current travels from the active electrode through the target and patient to the dispersive return electrode. In bipolar operation, current passes between two electrodes at the instrument tip.

FeatureMonopolarBipolar
Current pathActive electrode → target tissue → patient return path → dispersive electrodeBetween electrodes at the instrument tip
Return electrodeNormally requiredNormally not required for conventional bipolar use
AdvantageVersatile cutting and coagulation optionsLocalised current path
ConsiderationReturn-pad contact and alternate current pathsLocal thermal spread and instrument condition

Electrodes

Cutting electrodes include needle, lancet and loop designs. Their geometry affects current concentration and the precision of the tissue effect.

Ball and plate electrodes provide larger contact areas and are commonly associated with coagulation. Contact area, tissue properties, activation duration and electrode condition influence the outcome.

The large dispersive plate used in monopolar operation completes the return circuit.

Surgical diathermy analyser

A surgical diathermy analyser evaluates generator output using an appropriate load and measurement method. Depending on the analyser, it may measure output power, current, voltage, frequency and high-frequency leakage.

Measurements are compared with the manufacturer's specifications and applicable service tolerances. Only suitable instruments and approved procedures should be used for testing.

Measurement chain

  1. Generator output
  2. Specified test load
  3. Voltage and current sensing
  4. Measurement and signal conditioning
  5. Display and comparison with tolerance

Advantages, limitations and applications

Advantages

  • Rapid cutting and haemostasis.
  • Multiple modes and electrode designs.
  • Potential for output monitoring and feedback.

Limitations

  • Thermal spread cannot be eliminated.
  • Smoke may obscure the field.
  • Settings vary across devices.

Applications

  • General and specialty surgery.
  • Endoscopic procedures.
  • Equipment testing and maintenance.

Conclusion

A surgical diathermy unit is a controlled RF energy system. Its effects depend on waveform, electrode geometry, tissue response, current path and activation time.
